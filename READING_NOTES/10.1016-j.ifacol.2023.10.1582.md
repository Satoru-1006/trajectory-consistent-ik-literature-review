## Research question and contribution

The paper asks how known analytic singular configurations can be used to make joint-space trajectory optimization for serial manipulators avoid singular regions at lower computation cost than direct manipulability maximization. It derives robot-specific singularity conditions for a 6-DoF Comau Racer 7-1.4, a non-offset 7-DoF KUKA LBR iiwa 14 R820, and an offset 7-DoF Franka Emika Panda, then builds repulsive potential terms from those conditions. The authors describe the Comau and Panda analytic singularity sets as new to the best of their knowledge.

The contribution is analytical singularity characterization plus a low-cost penalty in an online-trajectory-optimization formulation. It is not an IK solution-branch labelling, continuation, or switching method. Its “trajectory optimization” chooses a joint-space path between joint-space endpoints; it does not track a supplied Cartesian curve whose inverse solutions form the branches under study.

## Robot model, singularity analysis, and trajectory formulation

- Each arm is modeled as a fixed-base serial rigid-body manipulator using Denavit–Hartenberg transforms. The task Jacobian maps joint rates to the six-dimensional end-effector twist. A configuration is singular when the Jacobian rank is below six. The paper uses Yoshikawa manipulability as a zero-at-singularity index and transforms the Jacobian into the end-effector frame to simplify symbolic analysis.
- For the 6-DoF Comau arm, row operations expose analytic conditions including (q_5=0), (q_3=\arctan(d_4/a_3)), and a further configuration-dependent condition given in Eq. (14c). For the 7-DoF arms, a transformed Jacobian is divided into blocks. The authors use the Cauchy–Binet theorem to express the determinant of the (3\times4) block’s Gram matrix as a sum of squared (3\times3) minors; this makes its rank-loss equations easier to analyze. Rank loss in the other block is treated as a necessary condition that must be checked against the full Jacobian.
- The appendix gives the KUKA conditions: (q_4=0); (q_2=0\land q_3\in\{-\pi/2,\pi/2\}); (q_2=0\land q_6=0); and (q_5\in\{-\pi/2,\pi/2\}\land q_6=0). The Panda conditions are stated as four implicit sets, involving combinations of (\sin(q_2)=0), (\cos(q_3)=0), (\cos(q_5)=0), (\sin(q_5)=0), and the appendix functions (f_{sing,1}(q_4,q_6)) and (f_{sing,2}(q_3,q_4,q_5,q_6)). The symbolic Panda manipulability expression is omitted because of space; the authors say the source file is available on request.
- Robot dynamics are (M(q)\ddot q+C(q,\dot q)\dot q+g(q)=\tau). The optimization assumes inverse-dynamics compensation, yielding the virtual double-integrator model (\ddot q=v). This is a modeling assumption; no torque limits or physical controller performance are tested.
- Direct transcription uses state (x_k=[q_k^T,\dot q_k^T]^T), virtual input (v_k), and final time (t_F). The objective sums final time, (v_k^Tv_k), and a singularity term. Initial and final states are fixed; in the experiment their joint velocities are zero. Box bounds are imposed on the discrete states (joint position and velocity) and virtual input (acceleration), with (t_F\ge0). These constraints are imposed at the (N) transcription points; the paper does not establish continuous-time satisfaction between them.
- The optimization is in joint space and connects sampled joint configurations. It does not prescribe a Cartesian pose curve, path tube, or task-space tracking error constraint, and it has no branch identity or branch-switch state.

## Singularity objectives

The comparison objective minimizes inverse manipulability, (L_{sing,1}(q)=w_m/(m(q)+\epsilon)), with (\epsilon>0). The authors explicitly say it does not strictly avoid singularities for all cases; sufficiently large weight is needed. They also criticize the cost of evaluating the manipulability expression and its derivatives and note that manipulability is a volume proxy rather than a direct distance to a singular set.

For the proposed objective, each analytically known singular set is represented by an implicit residual (\psi_i(q)) that is zero on that set. For an isolated single-joint condition, the example is a squared deviation ((q_m-q_{m,s})^2); for a two-joint condition it is a sum of two squared deviations. A repulsive term is (\phi_i(q)=\exp(\eta_1-\eta_2\psi_i(q))), and (L_{sing,2}(q)=w_m\sum_i\phi_i(q)). The terms are equally weighted. The authors describe this as a joint-space distance-based penalty with cheaper gradient and Hessian calculations. In use, it remains a finite soft cost in the objective, not a hard exclusion constraint or a certified positive distance from the singular set.

Table 2 sets (w_m=10^2), (\epsilon=10^{-6}), (\eta_1=\log(2400)), and (\eta_2=400). The reported minimum manipulability therefore depends on these selected weights; the paper does not state a hard lower-bound guarantee.

## Evaluation and results

For each robot, the authors run (10^4) Monte Carlo initial/target joint-configuration pairs, sampled uniformly over admissible joint ranges. Endpoint configurations with manipulability below (10^{-4}) are excluded. Each trajectory has (N=30) transcription points. The tests compare no singularity avoidance, inverse-manipulability maximization, and the proposed potential objective. IPOPT with MA27 solves the nonlinear program; CasADi supplies gradients and Hessians. The stated computer is a 3.4 GHz Intel Core i7 with 32 GB RAM. The problem has 631 variables for 7-DoF and 541 for 6-DoF. The reported timings are solver computation times for these small discretized problems, not a closed-loop controller or hardware benchmark.

Values below are mean ± standard deviation in the order **no avoidance / manipulability maximization / proposed potential**; (m_{min}) and (m_{max}) are aggregate extremes across the reported samples.

The potential method raises the observed minimum manipulability in all three sampled cases and reduces mean solver time relative to manipulability maximization, with the largest timing difference on the Panda. Manipulability maximization yields the highest average manipulability. Both avoidance costs increase average trajectory time over the no-avoidance baseline. Proposed-potential time is lower than manipulability-maximization time for KUKA and Panda, but **not** Comau (4.07 s versus 3.82 s). The potential method’s path length is lower than manipulability maximization for each robot, although it is not always shorter than the no-avoidance baseline.

The results are finite-sample, discretized simulation evidence. There is no real-time receding-horizon execution, robot hardware trial, continuous-time singularity check between grid points, reported branch-switch analysis, or hard-bound proof. The paper reports table averages and standard deviations, not a per-case solver-failure or continuous-feasibility audit. A large minimum manipulability in these samples supports the selected penalty’s empirical behavior; it does not establish universal singularity avoidance.

## Branch continuity, singularity transitions, and joint constraints

- **Solution branch:** No inverse-kinematics solution set is tracked along a supplied Cartesian path. The optimizer chooses a joint trajectory between joint endpoints. There are no branch labels, continuation predictors, branch matching rule, warm-start identity test, or branch-switch logic.
- **Singularities:** The method analyzes known singular configurations and penalizes proximity to them during trajectory optimization. It is an avoidance objective, not a method for crossing a singularity, changing between IK branches, or maintaining a selected solution through a singular transition. The soft objective does not guarantee singularity exclusion.
- **Joint constraints:** The discrete optimization bounds joint position and velocity and the virtual acceleration input. It is directly relevant to including joint constraints at trajectory nodes. It does not prove bound satisfaction continuously between nodes and does not include actuator torque limits or a reported robustness margin.
- **Task consistency:** There is no fixed task-space path to track; endpoints are random joint configurations. Thus the method should not be counted as evidence for trajectory-consistent inverse kinematics along a prescribed Cartesian trajectory.

## Quality and relevance to the review

**Full-text assessment: retain as adjacent/background evidence, not as direct evidence for branch tracking or singularity transitions.** The paper is relevant to fixed-base serial manipulators, robot-specific singularity analysis, potential-based avoidance, and discretized joint-space trajectory optimization with joint/velocity/input bounds. Its analytic conditions across three different kinematic structures are useful context, and its experiment is substantially broader than a single illustrative simulation.

The quality limits are the short conference-paper treatment, Monte Carlo simulation without hardware or receding-horizon execution, no continuous-time verification, no explicit random seed or per-instance outcomes, and omission of the Panda manipulability expression. The publisher version was not available for version comparison. The numerical comparison supports the proposed cost’s observed behavior under the stated setup, not a theorem or universal performance claim. The original `CORE_INCLUDE` label remains the title/abstract-stage decision; the full-text synthesis role is adjacent.

## Author-stated future work and evidence locators

The conclusion proposes integrating the method into real-time trajectory optimization for human–robot interaction with Cartesian compliance control and studying the qualitative path shapes. The paper does not state stronger guarantee or hardware limitations in a dedicated limitations section.
