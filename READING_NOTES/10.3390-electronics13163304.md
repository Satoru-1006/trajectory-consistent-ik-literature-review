## Research question and formulation

The paper presents a geometric inverse-kinematics construction for a 7-DOF SRS (3-1-3) anthropomorphic serial arm, followed by an objective for choosing among a finite set of configurations and separate APF/RRT planning sections. The abstract claims eight seven-joint vector solutions are obtained from Denavit–Hartenberg geometry. The redundancy parameter `α` determines self-motion posture; elbow and other inverse-trigonometric choices generate the candidate set. The model is simulated with MATLAB Robotics Toolbox and CoppeliaSim.

For one target pose, the selection objective is

`E(θ) = ωp ||P(θ)−Pd||² + ωr Error(R(θ),Rd) + ωs Σ(θi−θi+1)²`.

It weights position error, Frobenius-norm rotation-matrix error, and joint-angle smoothness among the finite candidate set `A`. The paper does not formulate this as continuation over a full Cartesian path or a search over a globally connected IK branch. The single-pose comparison table reports joint-angle differences below 0.01 rad from CoppeliaSim for one initial-to-target comparison.

## Branches, singularities, and constraints

The 7R candidate solutions are discrete vectors evaluated for a requested pose. The paper does not assign persistent branch labels, track candidate identity over trajectory samples, define a singularity transition, or reason about cuspidal/nonsingular branch changes. Its smoothness term is a weighted preference in the finite pose-solution choice; it is not a guarantee of inter-sample continuity or a continuous joint-space lift.

Joint ranges and velocity/acceleration/torque limits are not specified as hard constraints in the IK optimization. The APF section modifies attractive potential with quadratic and conic components and treats task translation/orientation components; RRT operates on joint-space waypoints with collision checks. These are separate general path-planning components, not a joint-constrained trajectory-consistent IK formulation. The paper gives no formal proof of APF convergence, branch continuity, singularity avoidance, collision clearance between all samples, or bounded joint derivatives.

## Evaluation and evidence

The inverse-solution validation compares one seven-joint target solution with a CoppeliaSim result and reports per-joint angle errors of 0.00010–0.00768 rad (all under 0.01 rad). The paper then shows illustrative APF path plots in an obstacle environment and generic RRT plots, including a waypoint example. No quantitative trajectory-tracking benchmark, path-continuity metric, branch-transition test, obstacle-clearance margin, ablation study, computation-time table, or physical robot experiment is reported. The conclusion says real-world implementation is future work.

The reference list includes relevant prior trajectory-consistent and redundant IK work, but citation breadth does not add validation to the method actually evaluated here. The article has no external funding, says data are available on request, and points to future real-world implementation; these details do not independently establish low publication quality, but the sparse validation means its empirical claims have limited weight.

## Authors' limitations and review assessment

The authors identify real-world implementation and further optimization as future work. The paper’s abstract also frames the motion-planning contribution as simulation in MATLAB/CoppeliaSim. It does not provide explicit limitations about branch identity or singularity transitions.

**Relevance:** at most background on 7R geometric IK candidate generation, weighted pose solution selection, and generic APF/RRT motion planning. **Full-text scope decision:** exclude from the core evidence set for trajectory-consistent IK. The method does not track a selected IK solution along a Cartesian trajectory, analyze singularity transitions, or provide hard joint trajectory guarantees. Keep the citation in the full-text audit trail so the initial abstract-screening decision remains traceable.
