## Research question and contribution

The paper asks whether splitting an industrial manipulator’s differential kinematics into translational and rotational subsystems can reduce the online cost of constrained nonlinear model predictive control while retaining joint and Cartesian constraints, obstacle avoidance, and end-effector orientation.

The architecture assigns the first three joints of a six-revolute-joint anthropomorphic arm to wrist-point translation and the last three spherical-wrist joints to orientation. A reduced NMPC controls the arm; a quaternion-error Jacobian-transpose controller controls the wrist. Before online control, an automatic inverse-kinematics configuration selector connects a measured starting configuration to one admissible terminal configuration with a seventh-degree joint-space polynomial.

## Robot, task, and kinematic decomposition

The only evaluation uses a fixed-base Stäubli TX2-90 six-DoF serial industrial robot, simulated in Gazebo and commanded through ROS. The end-effector follows a preplanned point-to-point motion while the controller adapts it to four spherical obstacles, including moving-obstacle scenarios. The examples include a downward-pointing tool orientation and optional wrist-point height limits.

The inverse-kinematics analysis computes the wrist point from the desired tool pose. For this nonredundant robot, the anthropomorphic arm can have up to four shoulder/elbow configurations, and the spherical wrist can have top/bottom solutions, giving up to eight pose solutions. In a redundant decomposition with more arm joints, a null-space condition would additionally be needed.

## Candidate selection and trajectory construction

The method removes terminal IK solutions outside the joint-position box. For each remaining solution, it constructs a seventh-degree polynomial for every joint between the current configuration and that terminal configuration. The endpoint conditions match joint position, velocity, acceleration, and jerk; the chosen initial/final acceleration and jerk are zero. The candidate trajectories are sampled to reject transitions with a jump at the shoulder, elbow, or wrist. Among the remaining candidates, the selector maximizes the worst per-joint distance to that joint’s lower or upper position limit over the samples.

This is explicit discrete IK-solution selection and jump screening for one initial-to-terminal PTP transition. It does not define a branch label or continuation rule for a sequence of arbitrary Cartesian waypoints, repeated loops, or branch changes at singularities. The polynomial is a continuous joint-space reference; it is not a proof that the online obstacle-avoiding NMPC remains on a formally tracked IK branch.

## Online NMPC, orientation control, and constraints

The translational OCP uses the first three joint velocities as bounded inputs and integrates the decomposed wrist-point Jacobian. Its running cost weights input magnitude, joint acceleration, and wrist-point tracking error. Direct multiple shooting discretizes the horizon. The stated constraints include:

- joint-position bounds for the translational arm joints;
- joint-velocity input bounds, plus acceleration and jerk bounds;
- optional Cartesian wrist-height bounds;
- spherical obstacle-distance constraints, with the obstacle radius inflated by the tool/wrist geometry.

The orientation controller uses the last three wrist joints, a unit-quaternion orientation error, and the transpose of the rotational Jacobian rather than its inverse. The authors derive a decreasing Lyapunov function and state that the controller is asymptotically stable and can cross kinematic singularities, with transient orientation deviation possible. The NMPC input regularizer is described as preventing singular arcs; a manipulability term can also be added. No singularity measure, branch-transition logic, or deliberate singularity-crossing test is reported.

The paper’s constraint coverage is not uniform across all joints: the arm’s position and motion bounds are formulated in the OCP, while Table 1 describes the wrist controller as bounded by its standard controller limits without giving an equivalent constrained optimization formulation. The obstacle envelope is centered on the wrist point and accounts for the end-effector geometry; the paper does not report a whole-arm collision or self-collision certificate. Torque limits and dynamic feasibility are not established by the kinematic OCP.

## Evaluation and results

All results are simulation evidence. The controller uses MATLAB, CasADi, IPOPT, ROS, Gazebo, and a Linux Ubuntu 18.04 computer with an Intel Core i7-8550U and 16 GB RAM. The full-system NMPC comparison uses a different objective formulation and six joint decision variables; the decomposed system uses three OCP decision variables and the separate orientation controller.

For 10 ms grid intervals, the reported mean solve times (decomposed vs. full system) were:

| Scenario | 100 ms prediction horizon | 200 ms prediction horizon |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| No obstacle | 26 ± 1 vs. 130 ± 12 ms | 30 ± 2 vs. 216 ± 21 ms |
| Obstacle | 27 ± 2 vs. 153 ± 53 ms | 33 ± 2 vs. 209 ± 9 ms |
| Obstacle and height constraints | 29 ± 2 vs. 230 ± 136 ms | 36 ± 6 vs. 284 ± 112 ms |

The authors report that the decomposed controller uses about 10–20% of the full-system computation time and has lower solve-time variability. The optimization was run until convergence using a stated objective tolerance; this is not a worst-case execution-time bound. The online tracking setup used a 100 ms prediction horizon, ten grid points, a four-step (40 ms) command buffer, and a 1.5 s transition.

The undisturbed example moves the tool from [110, −350, −405] mm to [780, 390, −405] mm. Figure 3 shows small transient tracking errors that converge near the endpoint, but the paper gives no scalar RMS or maximum position-error statistic. With four fixed obstacles, the controller either swerves upward or, with wrist height constrained to −228 to −145 mm, takes a planar detour. Two moving-obstacle examples use uniform obstacle velocity components of −30 mm/s in x and +45 mm/s in y, or move the central obstacle at −60 mm/s in x. The plots show the tool detouring, slowing briefly when the corridor closes, and reaching the endpoint after the corridor opens. No collision-rate statistic, measured clearance distribution, physical robot trial, or quantitative repeated-run analysis is reported.

## Branch, singularity, and constraint relevance

- **Solution branch:** Direct but narrow evidence. The method enumerates up to eight terminal IK configurations, filters out-of-bounds solutions, rejects sampled joint-polynomial transitions with shoulder/elbow/wrist jumps, and selects the remaining transition with the largest sampled joint-limit margin. It addresses a single PTP connection from a known starting configuration; it does not formalize general multi-waypoint branch tracking, loop closure, or certified continuity through singular configurations.
- **Singularity:** The arm NMPC regularizes joint velocity and optionally manipulability. The wrist controller avoids Jacobian inversion and uses quaternion coordinates, which avoid Euler-angle representation singularities. The authors claim it can cross kinematic singularities, but do not report a singularity metric or an experiment designed to characterize a singularity transition. These are distinct claims: avoiding an inverse-Jacobian calculation or Euler gimbal lock is not by itself a demonstrated IK-branch transition.
- **Joint constraints:** The OCP explicitly constrains the translational arm’s position, velocity, acceleration, and jerk, and selects the initial PTP branch for joint-limit margin. Wrist-joint limits are delegated to the standard controller in the architecture description. No torque-limit guarantee is provided. The Cartesian height and inflated spherical obstacle bounds are useful trajectory-control constraints, but do not establish whole-arm collision freedom.

## Full-text assessment and review relevance

**Full-text assessment: Core evidence, with a narrow scope.** Retain this paper as direct evidence for trajectory-aware selection among discrete IK configurations and for incorporating joint limits into a smooth point-to-point joint trajectory. Its online NMPC also shows how motion constraints can adapt a reference trajectory around obstacles. The paper does not solve the broader problem of explicit branch tracking through arbitrary Cartesian paths or provide singularity-transition guarantees, so those claims should not be attributed to it.

The method and implementation are described in detail, and the paper compares computational cost against a full-system NMPC. The evidence is a proof-of-concept simulation on one six-DoF robot; the comparison controllers use different objective functions, no external controller benchmark is included, numerical tracking-error summaries are absent, and no hardware trial is reported. The authors identify transfer to a real experiment and further solver/hardware improvements as future work. Data are available from the corresponding author upon request; no public code or dataset repository is identified.
