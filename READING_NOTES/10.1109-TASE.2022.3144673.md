## Research question and contribution

The paper constructs a configuration-space path for a serial manipulator that must follow Cartesian waypoints with relaxed pose tolerances and may use several discrete tool-center points (TCPs). This is intended for contact processes such as sanding, deburring, welding, painting, and composite layup, where the tool may have multiple useful contact locations and need not hold one exact six-dimensional pose at every waypoint.

The main contribution is an incremental, directed, layered graph planner. At each Cartesian waypoint, the planner samples the permitted pose/tolerance set and combines each pose with each available TCP to make flange frames. A robot IK solver maps these frames to candidate joint configurations. Valid configurations become graph nodes; compatible configurations at consecutive waypoints become edges. A Cartesian-space heuristic, forward/backward progression, and a cost-based source-selection bias seek low-cost paths while avoiding the cost of exhaustively evaluating the full graph.

## Robot, task, and kinematic assumptions

- The evaluated manipulator is an ABB IRB 2600, a six-DoF serial arm, modeled relative to its base frame. The tested setup is fixed-base; no base motion or redundant DoF is modeled.
- The inputs are a sequence of nominal Cartesian frames, waypoint-specific lower/upper bounds on position and orientation, a set of discrete TCPs, and a robot model/environment used for IK, joint-limit, and collision checks.
- Cartesian tolerance bounds are discretized by uniform sampling. For every waypoint, sampled transforms are combined with TCP definitions to create flange-frame sets. Duplicate frames are removed; an optional Euclidean-diversity filter in position/quaternion space reduces nearby candidates.
- Feasible configurations are discrete IK results at sampled frames and waypoints. The method does not parameterize a continuous inverse-kinematics solution manifold or follow a continuous Cartesian parameter with a differential IK integrator.

## IK graph, branch-family rule, and objective

Each graph level corresponds to one waypoint. The planner solves IK for candidate flange frames, checks each configuration against joint-position limits and collision constraints, and inserts valid solutions as nodes. It connects nodes at consecutive levels only when the maximum absolute joint change is below a threshold:

\[
\|q_{i+1}-q_i\|_\infty \leq \delta.
\]

The authors state that this bound is intended to keep the endpoint IK solutions within the same IK family. The edge cost and complete path objective are the Euclidean joint-space step length and its sum over the waypoint sequence:

\[
c_i=\|q_{i+1}-q_i\|_2, \qquad
J(\tau)=\sum_i \|q_{i+1}-q_i\|_2.
\]

A shortest-path search on the layered graph selects the least-cost discrete path among the evaluated nodes. The graph is built incrementally: a depth-first progression expands from source frames in the first and last waypoint sets, using Cartesian-nearest frames at succeeding levels; a subgraph search supplies a quality score for each source. The source-bias routine favors neighborhoods of lower-cost sources while eventually re-enabling and exploring sources it had disabled. If source sets are exhausted but intermediate frames remain, random sampling continues. The completeness statement is therefore resolution completeness with respect to the finite, discretized candidate frames, not completeness over the continuous tolerance volumes or continuous IK manifold.

After graph search, a discrete smoother inserts configurations near the current path to reduce path cost. It does not supply a time parameterization or a continuous dynamic-feasibility certificate.

## Singularity handling and joint constraints

- **Branch continuity:** This is direct but discrete evidence. The graph uses a componentwise joint-step threshold to discourage connecting different IK families between adjacent sampled waypoints. It does not maintain explicit shoulder/elbow/wrist labels, a branch state, or a continuation predictor. There is no stated threshold-adaptation or branch-transition rule at a singularity.
- **Singularities:** The introduction recognizes singularities as a general manipulator-planning concern. In the experimental setup, however, the authors assume the parts are within the robot's workspace and are not close to singularity. No Jacobian condition metric, singularity margin constraint, deliberate singularity crossing, or transition experiment is reported. Thus the article does not evidence singularity transition handling.
- **Joint position limits:** Candidate IK configurations are checked against joint limits at each sampled waypoint. The edge test additionally caps the maximum coordinate change between consecutive graph nodes. No joint velocity, acceleration, jerk, torque, or time-optimality constraint is enforced in this trajectory-generation method; the conclusion explicitly lists velocity, acceleration, and jerk constraints as future work.
- **Collision:** Nodes are checked with a hierarchical collision test, tool first and other environment objects if the tool is collision-free. The authors assume the robot remains collision-free while moving along an edge and identify swept-volume checking as an available extension. The edge itself is therefore not certified collision-free by the reported method.

## Cartesian heuristic and scope of its evidence

The heuristic uses the local relation \(\Delta x \approx J\Delta q\) to prioritize nearby flange frames at the next waypoint. The authors examine this relation using 30,000 random configurations and report it is approximately useful only for bounded Cartesian increments; the spread increases as the pose increment grows. They explicitly state that this relationship does not hold for a redundant robot with more than six DoF. When selected frames have no IK solutions, the planner falls back to the next-nearest candidate and reports no solution if the tested set remains invalid.

This is a search-order heuristic for a finite pose lattice, not a local inverse-kinematics continuation law. Its empirical rationale and performance evidence apply to the tested nonredundant six-DoF arm and sampled waypoint tasks.

## Evaluation and results

The evaluation uses five industry-inspired part/toolpath cases on the ABB IRB 2600. The tools include a sanding head with 15 discrete TCPs, a single-TCP deburring tool, and another sanding tool. The paths vary in waypoint count and angular tolerances; for cases A and B, bounds are reduced at 30 uniformly spaced waypoints to make the problem more constrained. There are no Cartesian position bounds in the test cases, and the authors assume the paths are reachable and away from singularities.

The comparisons are exhaustive graph construction and random sampling. The proposed planner is evaluated with and without source biasing, with 50-run averages reported for convergence profiles. The paper reports that random sampling does not find a solution for part D within 50,000 node evaluations, while the proposed method finds near-optimal solutions for all five cases with fewer evaluated nodes than exhaustive construction or random sampling. The text states the returned paths are within 1% of the exhaustive optimum after smoothing. Tables III and IV report evaluated/valid nodes and edges plus computation times; Table V reports before/after-smoothing cost ratios. Their numeric cells were not available in the browser text layer, so no per-case time or node counts are asserted here.

The evidence is algorithmic/computational evaluation on modeled industrial cases. The retrieved article text does not describe execution on a physical robot, repeated hardware trials, tracking error, or singularity-focused experiments.

## Relevance to the review

**Full-text assessment: retain as direct, bounded evidence for discrete path-wise IK family consistency under joint-position limits.** It is unusually relevant because it connects multiple IK solutions across a Cartesian waypoint sequence and explicitly uses a joint-space continuity threshold to avoid changing IK families. The result should be described as graph-level, discretized branch-family consistency, not continuous branch tracking. Its fixed-base six-DoF experiments align with the review's robot scope.

The paper contributes no explicit singularity transition mechanism, and its own experiments exclude near-singular cases. It also omits derivative and torque limits, and assumes collision-free motion between graph nodes. Those limitations make it a poor source for claims of complete trajectory feasibility, singularity passage, or dynamic constraint satisfaction. Keep the original title/abstract screening label `CORE_INCLUDE`, while using this narrower full-text interpretation in synthesis.
