## Research question and contribution

BoundMPC asks how a fixed-base manipulator can follow a user- or planner-specified Cartesian position-and-orientation path online while allowing controlled deviations in the orthogonal path directions, so the robot can respect kinematics, collision-free corridors and task constraints instead of being forced to track an unachievable Cartesian trajectory exactly.

The planner optimizes a joint-space jerk input inside a receding-horizon nonlinear MPC. It decomposes position and orientation path error into tangential progress and orthogonal error components; minimizes tangential error and penalizes joint motion, while constraining asymmetric orthogonal error within designer-provided bounds. Piecewise-linear Cartesian via-points make replanning cheap. The method is a path-following controller/trajectory generator; it does not compute or label the inverse-kinematics solution branches.

## Model and formulation

- Robot dynamics are represented as a rigid-body model, M(q)q̈ + C(q,q̇)q̇ + g(q)=τ. A computed-torque controller receives the planned q, q̇, q̈ reference and generates torque. The decision variable is joint jerk: piecewise-linear hat functions over a 10 Hz planning grid (the inner robot controller runs at 8 kHz in the reported setup). Integrating the jerk gives joint acceleration, velocity, and position over the horizon. The optimizer imposes bounds on state and input, i.e. joint position/velocity/acceleration and jerk, and tracks the Cartesian path through forward kinematics.
- The position error is projected onto the unit path tangent and its orthogonal plane. The two orthogonal components use basis vectors chosen from a desired direction and the path tangent. The orientation error is formed with the Lie logarithm of R_c R_rᵀ; left/right Jacobian approximations propagate it. It is decomposed into two cross-track axes and a tangential rotation component. This orientation approximation is locally linearized around an initial guess; the paper reports that its error grows later in a horizon, especially where reference angular velocity changes substantially.
- The MPC minimizes weighted tangential position/orientation error, error velocity/acceleration terms, distance from desired progress state, joint state regularization and jerk inputs. Path progress is optimized with bounded path velocity. Orthogonal position and orientation errors obey four scalar asymmetric constraints. Bounds can encode end-effector obstacle clearance corridors or application rules (e.g., keep a container upright).
- Bounds reach zero at via-points in the ideal profile, but small positive relaxations are used in practice to avoid numerical issues, especially for approximate orientation errors. Asymmetric bounds can become discontinuous at via-points unless carefully interpolated. The authors assume the task path and its error bounds are obstacle-free; the user or a higher-level planner must supply them.

A notable discussion states that bounded joint velocities can let a redundant arm pass through a kinematic singularity, which they reference as demonstrated in an experiment video. However, there is no branch identity state, singularity classification, branch map, or explicit branch transition policy. The reported experiment is an online path-following demonstration, not a controlled singularity-transition study. The claim must not be read as a proof that a selected IK branch remains continuous through every singularity.

## Replanning and implementation

For a path change inside the MPC horizon, the current Cartesian state is projected onto the new linear reference path; the path state is reset so tangential position error and its first two derivatives are zero. Orientation references and bounds are adjusted to keep the current state within the new corridor. This is a useful online reference-reset mechanism, but it can cause path-state jumps (the reported path speed can become negative at a replanning instant); it is not a solution-branch transition operation.

Implementation uses CasADi/IPOPT on a KUKA LBR iiwa 14 R820. The trajectory is sent at 10 Hz; the computed-torque controller interpolates to 8 kHz. The MPC horizon is 1 s in the main experiments. Parameter study reports for horizon N=10 mean solve time 39 ms and max 64 ms (N=30 mean 235 ms, max 848 ms); hence the long horizon improves the cost but loses the 100 ms real-time budget. The optimizer respects the discrete constraints; the paper does not provide an interval or between-sample proof for joint limits or corridor limits. ## Evaluation and reported evidence

Six application/tutorial scenarios are evaluated on the same 7-DoF iiwa: moving a large object through a constrained passage; opening a drawer; wiping a table; moving an object while avoiding obstacles around the full robot; online object-grasp replanning; and carrying an open container while keeping it upright.

For the large-object transfer, all three planners (JT-MPC, CT-MPC and BoundMPC) succeed in 10/10 runs in the initial setup. Mean geometric end-effector path length / trajectory duration are 1.59 m / 5.2 s for BoundMPC, 1.75 m / 5.0 s for JT-MPC, and 2.16 m / 8.2 s for CT-MPC; mean planning time is about 25 ms for BoundMPC. Thus it improves path length over these baselines in this task but is not the fastest in that table.

For the full-chain obstacle test, nine obstacle-shift configurations are evaluated. JT-MPC succeeds 0/9 and collides 9/9; CT-MPC succeeds 2/9, collides 1/9 and becomes stuck at obstacles or joint limits in the others; BoundMPC succeeds 9/9 without collision under the supplied collision-free bounds. This evidence depends on manually specified reference paths/bounds. In the random grasp-pose evaluation, all methods achieve 100% on 50 poses selected to be collision-free and reachable; mean trajectory durations are 4.1 s JT-MPC, 5.1 s CT-MPC, 5.1 s BoundMPC, and mean planning time about 30 ms for BoundMPC. In repeated randomized replanning, the MPC completes about 95 replanning steps per experiment with around 1.1% failure per step; the next chosen pose can be infeasible, so replanning is not guaranteed to succeed.

The open-container experiment bounds roll/pitch-like orthogonal orientation components to ±2.5° while leaving rotation about the upright axis less constrained. CT-MPC often violates the orientation requirement; exact path tracking can get stuck, whereas the bounded-error method finds a feasible deviation. The table-wiping scenario shows why a Cartesian path can matter: a joint-space tracking reference may not correspond to wiping the table. These are hardware demonstrations and task-level experiments, but not a blinded benchmark; path and bound design is partly manual, and comparisons depend on selected horizons, cost weights and tasks.

## Authors' limitations and evidence boundaries

The higher-level collision-free reference path and bounds are an input and their automatic generation is explicitly outside this paper; the paper says comparison timings omit the overhead of computing them. In the evaluated full-chain case, end-effector corridor bounds provide one layer while separate collision terms are needed for other links. Replanning uses a simple task-specific planner, not a general kinematics- and dynamics-aware global replanner. Orientation path errors use a first-order approximation, and their approximation error increases across a horizon after large orientation changes. The horizon/solve-time trade-off is material. There is no formal guarantee of continuous bound satisfaction, joint-limit margin, singularity crossing, or IK branch consistency.

## Relevance to the review

**Retain as adjacent evidence on trajectory-consistent Cartesian path following under joint and task constraints.** It is a strong example of an online joint-space MPC that keeps a Cartesian path as a reference corridor, allows principled deviations, enforces sampled joint position/velocity/acceleration/jerk limits, and replans. It helps frame how trajectory consistency can be expressed as a tube or path-error constraint. Its “trajectory consistency” is intentionally approximate: the end effector may leave the nominal curve within error bounds. It does not perform discrete IK solution-branch tracking or analyze general singularity transitions, and its input path/bounds are assumed safe. Do not use it as evidence of branch-continuity guarantees.
