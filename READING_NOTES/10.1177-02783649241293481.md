## Question and robot/task assumptions

The authors study how cuspidal 6R serial manipulators complicate inverse-kinematics solution identity and prescribed Cartesian path execution. A cuspidal robot can move continuously between distinct IK solutions without crossing a singularity. Consequently, a feasible joint-space lift can depend on the initial IK solution, and a closed Cartesian path can be continuous yet nonrepeatable.

The primary model is a fixed-base, generic 6R robot described by Denavit–Hartenberg parameters. The paper also illustrates the concepts on commercial Jaco Gen2 and FANUC CRX-10ia/L robots, compares wrist-partitioned architectures, and discusses UR5-like noncuspidal geometry. The task is a prescribed end-effector path with no arbitrary path deviation; the paper separates open paths, closed one-time paths, and repetitive closed paths.

## IK identity, branches, and singularities

The paper defines an aspect as a connected region of joint space without singular configurations and defines a nonsingular change as a path between IK solutions that avoids the singular set. Within one aspect, `sign(det J)` must agree, but that sign need not uniquely identify an IK solution in a cuspidal robot. The CRX/Jaco examples show why conventional shoulder/elbow/wrist configuration labels can be ambiguous or misleading. A missed IK root can cause a numerical solver to jump to another solution during Cartesian waypoint execution.

For fixed Cartesian paths, the authors classify path lifts by continuity and, for closed loops, repeatability. A cuspidal path can be feasible from one initial solution and infeasible from another. A continuous closed-loop lift may end at a different IK solution and then fail on repetition. For repetitive process work, they distinguish regular loops from one-time nonsingular solution changes. Their open-path framework samples the path, computes IK at each sample (an algebraic HuPf method is given as an example), selects a pose with few IK solutions, and checks connectivity from initial to intermediate and final solutions. Their closed-loop flowchart additionally checks task repeatability and whether a nonsingular change is acceptable.

## Cuspidality decision algorithm and guarantees

For simplified geometries, symbolic factorization of `det(J)` and known geometric results can identify multiple aspects; they analyze 832 simplified architecture types (about 5% of the enumerated 2^14 cases) and show why parameterized geometries can be classified without solving the generic problem.

For a generic 6R architecture, their practical algorithm discretizes the workspace and solves an optimal-path-planning (OPP) problem between two IK solutions of the same end-effector pose. The OPP trajectory maximizes the minimum signed Jacobian determinant along the path, subject to a smooth path state containing `q`, `q'`, and `q''`, with `q'''` as input. Joint bounds can be incorporated through geometric bounds. Revolute-angle representatives modulo `2π` must be considered; ignoring equivalent representatives can falsely make a nonsingular connection appear impossible. A found feasible nonsingular connection establishes cuspidality for that robot. Failure over the finite workspace grid does **not** establish noncuspidality: the result is explicitly inconclusive and depends on the selected workspace discretization. Their reported mean is about 10 ms for one HuPf IK solve and 8.52 s for one connectivity query on an Intel i7 12th-generation PC with 32 GB RAM.

The proposed cuspidality decision accounts for joint limits and internal link collisions conceptually, but the OPP equations shown explicitly bound joint/path derivatives and optimize distance from singularity; they do not provide a general dynamic/torque-constrained online IK tracker. The authors note that link-link collision constraints are nonsmooth and not algebraically expressible in the certified algebraic method. The paper's path-planning procedure is based on discretized poses plus numerical connectivity checks, not a continuous-time certificate for arbitrary input trajectories.

## Evidence and limitations

The authors analyze a 3D slice of the 14-parameter DH space, testing 3,240 robots; the method classifies the reported cases, including cuspidal regions near offset-wrist architectures. The paper catalogs ABB IRB 140/KUKA KR5 and UR5/UR10 as noncuspidal in the examined models, and FANUC CRX-10ia/L, Kinova Link6, and Jaco Gen2 as cuspidal; Jaco's reported 12 IK maximum is found by a 100,000-point low-discrepancy workspace search, not claimed as an exact global count. The evidence is kinematic/numerical and illustrative; no physical trajectory-tracking performance or timing-quality comparison is presented.

The paper states that a complete path must be known before planning. It therefore regards cuspidal robots as a poor fit for collaborative situations where the agent/environment causes an unpredictable path that must be replanned online. Future work includes a dedicated algebraic planning package and horizon planning for unknown environments.

**Relevance:** directly supports the review's distinction between IK solution/branch identity, singularity-free transition, initial-solution dependence, repeatability, and joint-limit effects. **Boundary:** it is a kinematic classification and path-feasibility framework rather than a real-time trajectory-consistent IK controller. Its generic cuspidality negative result is inconclusive under finite workspace sampling, and it provides no general velocity, acceleration, torque, or continuous-time tracking guarantee.
