## Research question and contribution

The paper asks whether a learned feedback controller can follow a prescribed Cartesian path without solving inverse kinematics separately at each path point. The authors frame conventional pointwise IK as computationally burdensome and non-unique for higher-DoF arms, and propose turning tracking into a sequential decision problem.

The method does not compute an IK solution set. It learns a policy from joint state and Cartesian tracking error to incremental joint commands, then uses a low-level PID torque controller. This is a path-tracking alternative intended to avoid inconsistent independent IK answers. Its possible continuity benefit is implicit: the policy receives the current joint state as part of the observation. The paper does not formalize that effect as a configuration-space branch or prove it.

## Robot, state, action, and reward

All reported robot tests use the V-REP PRO EDU 3.6.0 simulation platform: a planar 2R arm, a six-DoF UR5, and a redundant seven-DoF KUKA LBR iiwa 7 R800. The setups are modeled as fixed-base arms. No physical robot experiment is reported.

For the 2R feasibility test, the state includes joint positions and velocities, actual end-effector position, and the desired point. The action is a discrete direction that increments each joint by 0.05 degrees. A DQN policy tracks a periodic path induced by two joint motions.

For UR5, the 15-dimensional state contains six joint positions, six joint velocities, and three Cartesian endpoint errors. Position mode uses bounded joint-angle increments of ±0.05 rad; velocity mode uses bounded increments of ±0.8 rad/s. A conventional PID torque controller is underneath the learned policy. The SAC policy has two 200-unit ReLU hidden layers; the reported replay buffer is 10^6, discount factor 0.99, batch size 128, actor/critic learning rate 10^-3, entropy weight 10^-3, and soft-update coefficient 0.01.

The path-tracking reward in Eq. (15) is the negative Euclidean distance from the current endpoint to the selected target point, with target-point spacing n set to 1, 5, or 10. Episodes stop after 100 steps or when endpoint distance is within 0.05 m. One condition adds a +5 terminal reward at that threshold. Observation-noise training uses Gaussian noise scaled by 0.005. The paper’s problem statement describes cumulative reward in terms of tracking accuracy and energy, but the implemented reward equation is an endpoint-distance term; energy is evaluated separately later.

The seven-DoF KUKA test reuses the SAC architecture and reward settings, follows an arc path generated by a path-planning algorithm, and tests only velocity control with n=1. The authors also show training curves under multiple random seeds, but do not report a seed count or a statistical summary of the resulting tracking errors.

## Branch tracking, singularities, and joint constraints

- **IK branch:** There is no analytic, differential, or continuation IK representation in the learned controller, and no branch labels, solution-set tracking, or joint-space cycle-closure condition q(0)=q(s_end). Continuous Cartesian tracking in the simulator does not establish that a particular inverse-kinematic branch is followed.
- **Singularities:** The proposed policy does not use a Jacobian or define a singularity measure, singular set, approach policy, or transition rule. The experiments do not identify or deliberately cross singularities. Avoiding an explicit IK solver may avoid its numerical failure in a tested run, but it is not a demonstrated singularity-transition method.
- **Joint constraints:** Bounded per-step action increments are control bounds, not hard joint-position bounds. The paper does not formulate joint-angle admissibility inequalities or certify velocity, acceleration, jerk, torque, or collision constraints along the generated trajectory. The inner PID controller outputs torque, but that is not a stated torque-limit guarantee. UR5’s target path is generated in an obstacle scene, yet the learned reward and reported metrics do not establish collision avoidance by the policy.
- **Trajectory regularity:** The paper reports visually smooth tracking and defines smoothness using turning angles between adjacent curve segments. It does not establish continuity bounds for q, qdot, or higher derivatives, or a guarantee between sampled simulation steps.

## Evaluation and results

The 2R experiment is presented as feasibility evidence only. UR5 tests position and velocity modes, observation noise, target-point interval, and terminal reward. Tables 1–2 report mean path errors from 0.0318 to 0.0811 m across the listed settings; final endpoint distances range from 0.0030 to 0.0671 m. Increasing n to 10 generally trades path fidelity for better endpoint approach, while the +5 terminal reward often improves final endpoint distance but may worsen path error. The authors say noise during training improves robustness, but the tabulated tracking errors are mixed and do not show a uniform improvement across conditions.

Tables 3–4 vary the end-effector load from 0.5 to 5 kg while testing a fixed trained controller. Reported path errors change very little in these simulator runs. This is evidence for tolerance to the tested simulated load changes, not physical model-free generalization.

For UR5, Table 6 reports an energy metric of 4.44–9.16 for the learned variants and 8.95–13.32 for the “traditional Jacobian matrix” row across the listed load values. The paper gives the metric as a time-summed joint torque–velocity product, but does not clearly specify physical units or treatment of negative power. Table 5 compares a turning-angle smoothness measure. These comparisons do not evaluate branch continuity and the baseline details are limited.

The KUKA experiment is a simulation-based redundancy demonstration. Figures 9–10 show path tracking and training curves; the manuscript asserts stability across random seeds but does not give seed-wise numerical statistics or a hardware test. The data statement says supporting data can be requested from the corresponding author; no code or dataset repository is identified.

## Critical assessment and full-text relevance

**Full-text assessment: Adjacent/background.** This peer-reviewed 2023 publication is relevant because it explicitly motivates a learned path-tracking controller as a way to avoid inconsistent pointwise IK answers. It is not evidence for a trajectory-consistent IK algorithm: it bypasses IK and does not represent, track, or certify an IK solution branch. It also does not address singularity transitions or hard joint constraints. Keep it as an alternative path-control strategy and a useful contrast in the review, not as direct evidence for branch-continuous IK.

The simulation setup and hyperparameters are described well enough to understand the proposed controller, and the authors report task-error tables plus an energy/smoothness comparison. The evidence remains limited to simulation, one main UR5 scenario plus two demonstrations, and a narrow Jacobian baseline. The broad claims of robustness and generalization exceed the reported seed-wise and simulator-only evidence. The manuscript has no dedicated limitations section; the data are available on request.
